Privacy as method

Living people are not nodes in the public dataset. Consent to curate the project does not become permission to map an extended family online.

01 / The method

Privacy changes
the question.

A conventional genealogy page asks who belongs to whom. This exhibition asks what remains visible after identity is deliberately withheld. The family is not erased; the analytical lens moves from personal exposure to shared historical pattern.

01

Separate stewardship from lineage.

The project steward may be publicly credited by consent. That credit is never used as the first node in a chain connecting living and deceased relatives.

Consent is specific
02

Aggregate before publishing.

Generation, region, era, occupation and evidence class can reveal the shape of family history without publishing a searchable directory of people.

Pattern over exposure
03

Keep uncertainty intact.

Documents, family memory, working-tree leads and inherited traditions remain visibly different. An appealing story is not promoted into fact by repetition.

Uncertainty is information
02 / Translation

From family directory
to cultural record.

The private archive can retain names, exact relationships and source files. The public artifact publishes only what is needed to understand the history.

Withheld from the public layer

  • Living-person names and exact relationships
  • Exact birth dates, addresses and contact details
  • Raw GEDCOM files and database identifiers
  • Unredacted documents containing personal data
  • Direct chains from the steward to named relatives

What the exhibition can still reveal

  • Generational depth and branch survival
  • Regional migration across centuries
  • Evidence strength and evidence decay
  • Work, food, war and family-memory themes
  • Where and when the historical record goes dark

05 / Historical records

People appear where
the archive speaks.

Deceased individuals may be named inside a discrete historical record. They are presented as archival cases—not as rungs in a public family tree and not as clues to living relatives.

Newspaper record / Michigan / 1904

An industrial accident enters the family record.

A Muskegon Chronicle report documents that John Zeldenrust was badly injured while working on a logging railroad near Sturgeon Bay, Michigan. After efforts to save the limb failed, his right leg was amputated above the knee. The item anchors a remembered family event in a specific historical source.

Document-confirmed
Military archive / 1943–1945

A wartime service record becomes more precise.

Records for PFC Burton Leroy “Burt” Devowe document Army service, five European campaigns, a Purple Heart and a January 1945 wound/treatment entry. Family recollections of Utah Beach and the Remagen area remain oral-history leads pending unit-level records.

Records + memory separated
Aircrew archive / World War II

A crew role emerges from an original report.

A Missing Air Crew Report identifies S/Sgt Charles E. Swanson as assistant radio operator aboard B-17G 42-32065, 346th Bomb Squadron, 99th Bomb Group, and preserves eyewitness detail from the aircraft’s loss over Belgrade.

Document-confirmed
Family memory remains part of the archive.

Food, Sunday dinners, livestock hauling, storytelling and euchre survive as cultural evidence. In this public version, those memories are summarized by theme rather than attached to a living-family map.

06 / Evidence language

The archive shows
how it knows.

Genealogy becomes more trustworthy when certainty is visible. Four evidence classes prevent a database entry, a family recollection and an original record from being presented as though they mean the same thing.

Document-confirmedOriginal or official evidence

Military papers, vital records, newspapers and other records that directly support the claim.

Family-confirmedKnown within the family

Directly confirmed by the steward or close family and preserved as family knowledge.

Working tree leadA roadmap, not proof

Present in the GEDCOM or contributed trees and still requiring generation-by-generation review.

Family traditionA clue worth preserving

Royal, migration, court or military details that may guide research but remain unverified.

07 / The dataset asks

Better questions need
fewer exposed people.

Once identity is no longer the display’s organizing principle, the archive begins asking historical and methodological questions instead.

Q / 001 · Migration

How long does a branch remain in one region before moving?

Compare residence patterns by era and geography without publishing household-level routes.

Q / 002 · Evidence

At what century does each line become uncertain?

Track documentary thinning and distinguish missing records from unsupported imported claims.

Q / 003 · Language

What changes when a surname crosses languages?

Study Devowe / Deveau and other variants as migration evidence rather than as present-day locators.

Q / 004 · Labor

Which forms of work repeat across generations?

Group occupations into agriculture, transport, industry, domestic labor and military service.

Q / 005 · Memory

What survives in families that institutions never record?

Recipes, gathering rituals, humor and personal reputation form a second archive beside official documents.

Q / 006 · Absence

Whose lives are easiest—and hardest—to document?

Examine whether maternal lines, migrants, laborers or people whose names changed disappear sooner from the record.

Public archive rule

Publish the history,
not the keys to a person.

The public exhibition is deliberately narrower than the private research archive. Its omissions are not defects. They are part of the research design.

Ancestry does not require publishing a directory of people. Patterns of migration, survival, work, memory, language, conflict and uncertainty remain visible after identifying information disappears.

No living-person lineage nodes
No exact living-person dates
No private addresses or contacts
No raw GEDCOM download
No sensitive record identifiers
No unredacted personal documents
